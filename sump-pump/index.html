A sump pump moves groundwater and stormwater from a pit to the outdoors. An ejector pump moves sewage from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the sewer line, and it has a sealed lid and a different pump built to pass solids. They are not interchangeable, and each needs its own pit.

If your basement floods during storms, it is usually the best addition you can make, because storms are exactly when the power goes out. A backup pump runs from a battery for a limited number of hours, so it buys time rather than replacing the primary. Sizing depends on how much water your pit receives.

There is water on the basement floor after the rain. Why did the pump not catch it?

Either the pump failed, or it could not keep up. A dead motor, a stuck float, a clogged intake or a power outage all leave the pit to overflow. A pump that runs flat out and still loses is simply too small for the inflow, which a plumber can tell by watching how fast the pit fills.

Sump pump installation and repair means getting the pit, the pump, the check valve and the discharge line working as one system, so water leaves the house as fast as it arrives.

Emergency steps

What to do when the basement is taking on water right now

Water on a basement floor can reach outlets, the furnace, the water heater and the electrical panel. Safety comes first, and the plumber dispatched around the clock works from that same order.

  1. Do not walk into standing water if outlets, power strips or appliances are in it. If the panel is in the basement and the water is rising near it, call the electric utility from upstairs.
  2. Check the outlet. A tripped GFCI is a common cause of a pump that seems dead, but only reset it if you are standing on a dry floor.
  3. Unplug the pump first, then lift the pit lid and look. If the float is stuck against the wall, free it, plug the pump back in and watch it run.
  4. Move valuables, boxes and electronics onto shelves or upstairs, and use a wet vacuum or buckets to slow the rise.
  5. Call, and tell the dispatcher about the water depth and any smell. A sewage odor means the water is not groundwater.

Primary pump versus battery backup

Most protected basements use both. They do different jobs.

A high-water alarm on the backup warns you before the pit overflows.

The check valve, the pit and the float

The check valve sits on the discharge pipe above the pump and stops water from running back into the pit after each cycle. Without it, the pump re-pumps the same water over and over until it burns out. A rattling thump when the pump stops is the valve closing, and silence with a pump that short-cycles often means it has failed.

The pit should have a liner, a solid bottom and a lid. Silt that washes in jams impellers, and a float that scrapes the wall will not switch on. These small fixes cost less than a new pump.

Where the water goes: discharge rules

Discharge belongs outdoors, with the pipe ending several feet from the foundation and sloping away from it. If the water returns to the house, the pump cycles endlessly.

Sewer connections are a different matter. The Braintree Water and Sewer Department says sump pumps must discharge away from the sewer, and we mention it as an example of a common municipal rule, not as Milton law. Ask Milton Water and Sewer before connecting anything to a sewer, and the Milton plumbing inspector can say what permits new piping needs.

Plumbing work around the pump follows the Massachusetts Uniform State Plumbing Code, 248 CMR 10.00, the standard the local inspector applies.

Ejector pumps are a separate machine

If a basement bathroom, laundry or utility sink sits below the sewer line, an ejector pump lifts the waste up to it. It sits in a sealed basin with a vent and moves solids that a sump pump would choke on. Ejector installation is planned work, and the plumber sizes the basin and routes the vent. If the sewer line itself is the trouble, a camera inspection helps.

What a new sump pump installation involves

Planned installs follow a steady order.

  1. Walk the basement

    The plumber finds the low point, checks existing drains and decides where the pit should go.

  2. Cut the floor and dig the pit

    A section of slab is opened, the soil removed and a liner set in the hole.

  3. Set the pump and float

    The pump sits on a firm base, with the float clear of the walls.

  4. Run the discharge

    Pipe with a check valve leads outdoors and ends away from the foundation.

  5. Test under load

    Water is poured in and the plumber watches the pump cycle, the valve and the discharge.

What affects the price of a sump pump job

Pricing is stated up front, before the pit is touched. Here is what changes it.

New pit or existing pit

Breaking the slab and digging a pit adds labor that a swap in an existing pit does not need.

Pump capacity and type

Higher flow, cast bodies and ejector models cost more than basic units.

Battery backup and alarm

The backup pump, battery and controller add parts and setup time.

Discharge route

A long run, a trench or a wall penetration takes longer than a short exit near the pit.

Insurance note: sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, while groundwater seepage and flood damage may need a separate policy. Read your policy or ask your insurer.
