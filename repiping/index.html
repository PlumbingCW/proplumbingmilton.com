Rusty or brown water, low pressure and pinhole leaks: are the pipes at the end of their life?

Those three symptoms often travel together. Old steel pipe rusts from inside, the rust narrows the bore and flakes into the water, and thin spots eventually open into pinholes. Repiping is the full replacement that ends that cycle, instead of patching one failure after another.

Signs your supply pipes are wearing out

Several of these at once is a strong case for replacement.

  • Brown or orange water at the tap, especially first thing in the morning.
  • Water pressure that has dropped over the years at several fixtures.
  • Pinhole leaks or green and white crust at pipe joints.
  • Pipes that look flaky, swollen or stained with rust.
  • Repair after repair on the same line.
  • Metal taste, or hot water that runs weaker than cold.
  • A plumber noting galvanized steel pipe during an unrelated job.

What the MWRA says about old pipes and brown water

The MWRA, the only source of water for Milton, notes that old water pipes can develop tuberculation, a buildup of corrosion that can cause an occasional red, yellow or brown color in water. Discolored water that comes from the tap in your house but not a neighbor's points toward your own aging pipe.

Why Milton homes reach this point

In the Housing Production Plan's count, 46.8% of homes pre-date 1940, 28.5% went up between 1940 and 1959, and the median age is 71 years. In a house that old, original steel supply pipe may still be in the walls, or a patchwork of steel, copper and newer sections.

That mix tells you the work may be larger than it looks. Replacing a single bathroom run while the rest is original steel can leave you with new pipe attached to old, and the old pipe becomes the next leak. Ask the plumber to describe all the pipe, not only the section that failed.

Lead service lines: the Town side and the owner side

The Town of Milton maintains the water service line from the street main to the curb stop valve. Past the curb stop valve, the line to the house belongs to the owner. Milton has built its own lead service line inventory, and a replacement plan went to MassDEP in October 2024.

If you repipe inside the house, ask what the line looks like where it enters and whether the owner side is lead. Raise it with Milton Water and Sewer before the interior work starts, so the two projects can be planned together.

Copper or PEX: comparing the two common choices

Both are widely used for supply lines. What your house may use is decided under the Massachusetts Uniform State Plumbing Code (248 CMR 10.00) and by the local plumbing inspector.

Phased work or the whole house at once

A whole-house repipe replaces every supply line in one project. It ends the leak risk and is usually more efficient, because the plumber opens each area once. Phasing spreads the work over time: kitchen and bathrooms first, the rest later. It keeps the cost spread out, but it means opening walls more than once and living with old pipe in the meantime.

The right choice depends on the pipe condition, your budget and how much disruption you can accept. Ask for both options in writing with the clear price before work starts.

Wall access and how much repair follows

The plumber looks for the shortest path to each fixture: unfinished basements, attic floors, closets and the backs of cabinets. Where none exist, small openings are cut at the fixture and at points along the run. Plaster and tile are harder to patch than drywall, so ask how each opening will be handled.

Patching and painting are not always part of the plumbing scope. Ask who handles them, and whether the price includes them, before you agree.

What to expect, day by day

A whole-house job usually takes several working days. The plumber sets the actual schedule after seeing the house.

  1. Day one: walk-through and permit

    The plumber maps every fixture, names the pipe route, pulls the permit and protects floors and furniture.

  2. Next days: open and run new lines

    The main is shut off for set windows, access holes are cut and new pipe is run to each fixture.

  3. Connect and test

    New pipe is tied into the main and fixtures, and the system is pressure tested before anything is closed up.

  4. Inspection

    The local plumbing inspector checks the work, which is why the permit matters.

  5. Restore and flush

    Water is turned back on, aerators are cleaned and the lines are flushed to clear debris.

Permits and licensing

Only a licensed plumber can pull the repiping permit, and you can check a license and discipline history in the state eLIPSE portal or by calling the Board at 617-727-9952. That code is the standard a plumber's piping work is held to.
